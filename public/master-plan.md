# Semester I — Strategy Before Branding

This is where I want you to begin.

One of the biggest weaknesses I see in the branding world is people jumping immediately into:

mission → values → archetype → personality → moodboard → logo.

That's not enough.

# Semester V — The Big Marketing Science Debate

This is where your education becomes much more sophisticated than most "brand strategists."

You need to understand a major ideological divide.

## Traditional brand management

Keller / Aaker / Kotler:

Build strong differentiated brand meaning, customer relationships and brand equity.

VERSUS

## Ehrenberg-Bass / Byron Sharp

Read:

**How Brands Grow**

Then:

**Jenni Romaniuk — Building Distinctive Brand Assets**

This school emphasizes **penetration, mental availability, physical availability, category entry points and distinctiveness**.

The institute's research argues that growth comes primarily from expanding the buyer base, while recognizable brand assets and associations with buying situations help increase mental availability. 

Romaniuk's work is particularly important for you because it connects directly to design.

A distinctive asset must become both strongly associated with the brand and distinctive from competitors; assets can include color, typography, characters, shapes, sonic devices and other cues. 

That means:

**Logo design isn't merely decoration.**

You're creating **memory structures.**

That's a much more powerful way for L&H to talk about identity.

# Another major debate: differentiation vs distinctiveness

Study both sides.

Traditional strategy says:

> Be meaningfully different.

Ehrenberg-Bass often emphasizes:

> Be easily recognized and easily bought.

Kantar's research occupies somewhat of a middle position with its Meaningfully Different framework.

Your job as strategist isn't to become religious about either camp.

Your job is to ask:

**Which theory best explains this particular business situation?**

That's graduate-level thinking.

# Semester VII — Marketing Effectiveness

Read:

**Les Binet & Peter Field — The Long and the Short of It**

Their work distinguishes long-term brand building from short-term sales activation and argues that businesses need both. Their original general-market finding centered around roughly a 60:40 balance, although the correct allocation varies with context. 

Importantly, this isn't old trivia.

Their work remains active, and the IPA's 2026 research again warns against excessive optimization around narrow short-term metrics at the expense of business growth.

# WEEK 1 — STRATEGY

We're officially starting now.

### Essential question

> **What is strategy, and how is it different from goals, tactics, branding and operational improvement?**

Your four thinkers this week:

**Richard Rumelt**

Good strategy =

**Diagnosis → Guiding Policy → Coherent Actions**

**Michael Porter**

Strategy requires choices about **where/how to compete**, including what you deliberately *won't* do.

**Roger Martin**

Strategy is an integrated set of choices:

Winning aspiration → Where to play → How to win → Capabilities → Management systems.

**Theodore Levitt**

Businesses frequently define themselves around the thing they currently produce rather than the customer need they exist to serve.

# Lesson 1: Your first misconception to destroy

Suppose someone tells you their strategy is:

> "Become Milwaukee's leading luxury wellness brand."

That's **not strategy**.

It's an aspiration.

Or:

> "Post consistently on Instagram."

Tactic.

Or:

> "Provide excellent customer service."

Operating principle.

Or:

> "Refresh our logo and website."

Execution.

Strategy explains:

**What's happening → where we're choosing to compete → how we expect to win there → what we're going to do because of that choice.**

This distinction will become extremely important during client discovery.

Because clients will often come to you saying:

> "We need a new website."

Your response internally should be:

**Maybe.**

The website is the client's prescribed solution.

Your job is to diagnose the underlying problem.

That is the beginning of becoming a strategist instead of an order-taker.

# Your first case

Imagine:

**Mabel & Stone**

Milwaukee-area interior-design company.

12 years old.

Founder started designing middle-class residential renovations.

Business has grown substantially.

Projects that used to average **$20,000** now average **$150,000**.

They've started getting lake homes and architect-designed renovations.

They have excellent referrals.

Their original branding still consists of:

script logo
 sage green
 Squarespace template
 "Beautiful spaces you'll love to live in."

Instagram performs reasonably well.

Customers love them.

Founder tells L&H:

> "I think we've outgrown our website. I want something that feels more luxury."

A designer hears:

**luxury website.**

I want you learning to hear:

**something changed inside this business that the outside world no longer communicates.**

Your first questions should therefore be about:

customer
 business model
 competitors
 pricing
 reputation
 future ambitions
 lead sources
 project selection
 perception

**before fonts and colors ever enter the conversation.**
